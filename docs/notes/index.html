Notes: dimension and interpolation

Two words that cause most of the confusion around triangulation, and the wording the rest of this site uses for them. A third word, "regular", gets the same treatment for gridded data in the companion book Regular in what?.

Dimension

"2D" and "3D" mean different things in different communities: a "3D mesh" is a surface of triangles to a graphics programmer and a volume of tetrahedra to a finite-element analyst. Two meanings matter here.

  • Coordinate dimension: coordinates per vertex. xy is 2, xyz 3, xyzm or xyzt 4.
  • Topological dimension: the dimension of the shape. Point 0, segment 1, triangle or polygon 2, tetrahedron 3; a k-dimensional cell is a k-simplex.

The topological dimension never exceeds the coordinate dimension and is often smaller: a triangle in xyz is a piece of surface. A triangulation is always made of topologically 2-dimensional cells, whatever its coordinates.

"2.5D" always means one coordinate more than the shape uses, but which shape varies. Here it means the terrain sense: triangles built from xy with z carried, so z is a function of x and y. In fdaPDE it is any surface in xyz.

Where mixing them up goes wrong:

  1. Delaunay of xyz points is not a surface. It gives tetrahedra filling the hull. For a terrain, triangulate xy and carry z; for a sphere, take the convex hull (below).
  2. Refinement bounds are measured in the coordinates the mesher sees. With z carried, a maximum area bounds the xy footprint, not the surface; in longitude and latitude, areas are in degrees squared and distort towards the poles.
  3. Degenerate input lowers the dimension. Collinear points have no triangles: CGAL reports dimension() == 1, Qhull stops unless joggled.
  4. Attributes are not coordinates until you make them so. Elevation or time carried on vertices is interpolated onto new vertices but plays no part in where vertices go.

This site writes xy, xyz and so on for coordinates, names shapes (triangles in xy, triangles in xyz, tetrahedra in xyz), uses 2.5D only in the terrain sense, and avoids bare "2D" and "3D".

Triangles on the sphere

For points on a sphere or ellipsoid, without constraints or refinement: convert to geocentric xyz ("+proj=cart" through reproj, PROJ, gdalraster, sf or terra; or x = cos(lat) cos(lon), y = cos(lat) sin(lon), z = sin(lat) on a sphere) and take geometry::convhulln(xyz). Every point of a convex surface is a hull vertex, so the hull's facets triangulate the surface, and on a sphere they are exactly the spherical Delaunay triangulation. Vertex order is kept, so map back by index. Regional data gets a facet spanning the uncovered part; drop it by facet normal or maximum edge length. With constraints or refinement, use fmesher or tulpaMesh, or project to a local planar CRS first.

Interpolation

A triangulation is most often built to carry values. The word covers several jobs that share little: scattered data to a surface (the main one here); resampling a grid (bilinear is not triangle-linear, and splitting cells into triangles depends on the diagonal); attributes onto vertices a mesher adds; interpolating a coordinate transformation across a mesh (texture reprojection, GDAL's approximate transformer); and areal interpolation of totals between polygons (sf::st_interpolate_aw()), which interpolates no point values at all.

Linear on a triangle

Inside a triangle the value is w1 v1 + w2 v2 + w3 v3, with barycentric weights. Across the mesh this is a piecewise linear surface. It is exact for planes, continuous, and never overshoots the data, but its gradient jumps across every edge and the answer depends on the triangulation: the centre of a square differs by diagonal. Delaunay gives the least rough surface of any triangulation of the same points (Rippa, 1990). Triangle methods stop at the hull unless told to extrapolate.

Size and shape

Along an edge of length h, the error is at most h^2 / 8 times the largest second derivative, so a tolerance becomes a target size wherever the curvature is known; that is what a sizing field such as laridae's is for. The value error depends on size; the gradient error depends on the largest angle (Babuska and Aziz, 1976). Small angles alone are harmless, so a long thin triangle along a ridge can be a good one, though isotropic meshers will avoid it.

The mesh decides the answer

  1. Constraints place edges, not jumps. A breakline stops triangles straddling it, but the surface is still continuous across it. A real discontinuity needs two vertex rows at the same xy, one per side.
  2. Contours make flat triangles whose three vertices share a value, terracing the surface. Refinement cannot fix that; it needs extra information.
  3. Steiner points add triangles, not information, when the attribute is a measurement: their values are interpolated from the triangle they fall in (Triangle, cdtr and laridae do this; trowel interpolates from the mesh before refinement). When the attribute can be computed anywhere (a transformation, a raster you can sample), new vertices should be evaluated instead, and refinement does add information.
  4. Crossings can have two answers, one along each segment. The mesh spec does not yet say which wins.
  5. Remove outside triangles first. Interpolating across a hole or between polygons of a coverage mixes values that should not mix.

Linear in which coordinates?

Barycentric weights are linear in the coordinates the mesh was built in. Longitude and latitude, a projected CRS and geocentric xyz give different triangles and different weights; the differences are small for short edges and large across a continent, near a pole or across the antimeridian. Choose the coordinates on purpose. Transforming there and back is one PROJ call each way, and geographiclib vectorises projection centres, so a local projection per feature costs no more than a global one.

For a reprojection mesh, test a point in each triangle against the exact transformation and split where the error exceeds a tolerance; rims, poles and seams are better inserted as constraints than found by refinement.
